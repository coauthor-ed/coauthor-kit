# College audience: the card

Briefs quote this card. Selected by `profile.json` `"audience": "college"` or a course's `bundle.json` `"audience"` (`packs/audience/README.md`). Each item overrides the doc it names: where this card and that doc disagree, this card wins. Everything else in the kit still holds.

1. Reader (`docs/voice-and-tone.md` Reader). Undergraduate and postgraduate students and working professionals. Fluent in English, may read on a phone. Not CBSE, not classes 9-12: drop school, exam-board and teen framing.
2. Register (same doc, Reader and Register). Clear academic prose. A technical term is fine once defined, and still goes in `glossary.json`. No mascot voice, no cheering, no exclamation marks. Second person and present tense still hold. Clarity beats brevity: give the full explanation a first-time learner needs (`docs/pedagogy.md`).
3. Examples (Reader, Home ground). Indian context is welcome, not required: national surveys, Indian data and rupees sit beside international datasets. Name the place, year and population.
4. Prose limits (Rules 1; `tools/prose-lint.mjs` keys them on the audience id `college`; `profile.json` holds no limits): warn on a sentence over 35 words, a paragraph over 6 sentences, a beat over 160 words. The staccato rule is kept.
5. Pedagogy (`docs/pedagogy.md`). Open with inquiry and use real dead ends, as before. A `poe-predict@1` is still good and still one per lesson at most, but optional per lesson.
6. Games: the course's look card sets the quota (`plain`: mini-game optional, no arcade; `quill`: `docs/mini-games.md` and `docs/arcade-games.md` as written). Under any look a `mini-game@1` is written as an exercise (`docs/mini-games.md` "Exercise, not game"): no game, play, level, score, badge or challenge wording in learner-visible text (`prose-lint` flags it).
7. Questions (`docs/descriptive-questions-and-cbse-marks.md`). Its CBSE parts do not apply: no 2/3/5-mark bands, no board model-answer phrasing. Keep its `semantic-descriptive@1` mechanics (rubric, `level3`, `mode`, `answerMode`, the rubric checks). Size a rubric by the distinct points a correct answer needs.
8. Question mix (`docs/question-bank-generation.md`, sections 3.4 and 8.1). Favour data interpretation where the subject has data: `numeric-value@1` for a computed value, `comprehension-set@1` around one table or dataset. A question's `graph` draws functions and points only: a bar chart or histogram goes in an image or a simulation. The subject pack, if any, names the field's question shapes. Language level: the course's own register, not a class grade.
9. Sources (`docs/copyright-and-sourcing.md`). Cite each dataset and paper a slide or question uses in the source notes (`FIGURES.md`, `GENERATION-LOG.md`): author, year, title, table or figure, licence. Numbers in a stimulus come from the cited source or are labelled illustrative.
10. Review (`briefs/stageR-critique.md`, Check: pedagogy). Judge the age level against this card, not a CBSE class.
11. `bundle.json` `grade`: optional; omit it, or use a short level, `"UG"` or `"PG"` (no number range, no class), which the app shows as-is. Put the full level in `description` and `tags` ("postgraduate", "MSc").
12. Visuals (AGENTS.md rule 11). Every slide has something to look at (a scene, `graph`, `diagram`, image, pull quote or numbers table); never prose alone. Each lesson has at least 2 scenes the learner operates and sees a consequence in (a slider, a split, a fill, a sort), and its central idea is one of them. Every scene passes the necessity test (`docs/pedagogy.md` "Earn every scene": `Shows:` / `Without it:` on its outline line); a slide that fails is Text only with one emphasis device. Numbers follow `docs/numericals.md` (worked rows, stacked fractions, entry with a check; controls that look actionable).
13. Appeal. Many adult subjects (epidemiology, statistics, economics, law) read as dry; treat appeal as a requirement, not polish. Each lesson:
   - Stakes first. Open on a real case with human consequence (who was harmed, what was at stake), told plainly and with dignity, then the question it raises.
   - Concrete before abstract. Show the actual people, events or money (dots sorting into cells, a timeline filling) before the letters, formula or term.
   - Do, then name. The learner makes the move (predict, sort, slide, fill) before the slide names the concept.
   - One visual metaphor per key idea, moving: a ratio as two bars, bias as a tilted sample, time direction as an arrow that runs forward or back. Not boxes with words.
   - A payoff moment: the key number lands large, from the learner's own action.
   - A running thread across lessons (a live case, a dataset, a decision) that visibly advances each lesson.
   - Variety: no 3 slides in a row of the same kind; no 2 scenes in a row of the same family.
   - Test: would a learner show this slide to a friend? A correct but dull slide fails review (`briefs/stageR-critique.md`).
14. Question rhythm. Questions and predicts together at most about 2 in 5 slides of a lesson, never two in a row, and never a whole lesson of "read, MCQ, read, MCQ" (a reviewed course had one after every content slide and read as quiz fatigue). Prefer a predict, an operated scene or a worked entry over another MCQ; a question slide is a two-column prompt and answer, never a stem with half the slide empty.
15. Variety of form. No scene recipe (rule table + chips + two bars + a big number) more than twice per lesson; across the course the scenes look like different machines of one world. A `figure` is never words in ruled boxes: each item is a picture or a number first, a short label second.
16. One world. A course picks ONE visual grammar for its subject and keeps it in every lesson (epidemiology: one dot = one person; filled = exposed, blue = ill), so lesson 4 builds on lesson 2's picture. Each lesson lands one payoff number large, from the learner's own move.
17. Clear before clever. Each term is defined where first used, in the prose and (narrated) in the voice, with the slide's own numbers; every number on screen shows where it comes from; a derived quantity (a gap, a ratio, a P value) is built in front of the learner before it is plotted, and plotted as dots on a number line before any histogram. No aphorisms in place of teaching. Re-read every scene as a first-time learner: if the reviewer has to ask "what does the filled dot mean here?", the scene fails.
18. Narration (`docs/narration.md`). A college course may be narration-first and used by a lecturer to present in class. Decide it before Stage 1b (COURSE-BRIEF), so the outline, stubs and scenes are narration-ready.
