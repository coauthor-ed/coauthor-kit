# A Village Becomes a City

<!-- STEP id="village" -->
## A Handful of Villages

Picture mud-brick huts along a seasonal stream, grain stores, a cooking fire, herders bringing cattle in at dusk.

**A civilisation that would one day cover 1.5 million square kilometres started from a handful of scattered farming villages.**

<!-- STEP id="phases" -->
## Three Phases, One Region

Archaeologists group this growth into three phases:

- **Pre-Harappan** (7000-3300 BCE) - early farming villages, like Bhirrana and Kunal.
- **Early Harappan** (3300-2600 BCE) - fortified towns, like Kot Diji and Rehman Dheri.
- **Mature Harappan** (2600-1900 BCE) - planned cities, like Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira.

**Each phase built directly on the one before it - nothing about Harappan cities appeared from nowhere.**

<!-- STEP id="bhirrana" -->
## Bhirrana: Proof of Deep Roots

One of the oldest Pre-Harappan villages, **Bhirrana** in modern Haryana, dates back to 7000-5500 BCE - a mud-brick pit-dwelling village, not yet a city.

Villagers there made distinctive **Hakra Ware** pottery. One potsherd carries a scratched drawing of a dancing girl, centuries before the famous bronze statue from Mohenjo-daro.

**Long before any city existed here, Bhirrana's villagers were already skilled potters and artists.**

<!-- STEP id="kunal" -->
## Kunal: A Village's Hidden Wealth

Nearby **Kunal**, from the same era, has given up two silver tiaras, copper arrowheads, a metal-working furnace, and over 12,000 semi-precious beads.

The tiaras are the oldest known precious-metal crowns in the Indian subcontinent.

**A village this small already had metalworkers skilled enough to shape silver into crowns.**

<!-- STEP id="bricks" -->
## One Brick, Everywhere

By 2600 BCE, something changed: sites hundreds of kilometres apart started building with the exact same brick.

Every Harappan brick - baked or sun-dried - kept the same ratio: 1 part height to 2 parts width to 4 parts length (7 cm : 14 cm : 28 cm).

- Builders alternated **headers** and **stretchers** course by course, an interlocking bond that resisted the shear cracks monsoons and earthquakes cause.
- Fired bricks lined baths, drains, and city walls, resisting moisture decay for over four thousand years.

**The ratio is so structurally sound that civil engineers still build with the same 1:2:4 proportion today.**

<!-- STEP id="citadel" -->
## The Citadel

At Mohenjo-daro, planners split the city into two halves: a raised, fortified **Citadel** in the west, and an open **Lower Town** grid in the east.

The Citadel stood on a 12-metre mud-brick platform, tall enough to stay above Indus floods. It held the **Great Bath** - a sealed, bitumen-waterproofed pool about 12 m × 7 m × 2.4 m - a ventilated **Granary** to keep stored grain from rotting, and a pillared assembly hall on 20 stone columns.

**A raised citadel kept the city's most important buildings safe from the Indus floods below.**

<!-- STEP id="lowertown" -->
## The Lower Town

Streets in the Lower Town ran on a strict north-south, east-west grid, along nine-metre-wide boulevards.

Every house connected to a covered drain with a limestone inspection sump, and drew water from wells shared by the whole neighbourhood - a scale of sanitation no other ancient civilisation, not even Egypt or Mesopotamia, matched.

**A Harappan city wasn't just planned - it was engineered, down to its sewers.**
